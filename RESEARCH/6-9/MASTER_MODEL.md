# 6–9 — Competence & Mastery

## Status
Research foundation v1. This document is a model for corpus construction, not a diagnostic framework.

## Core developmental question
> Can I do hard things?

## Developmental thesis
Middle childhood is a period in which children increasingly organize their self-concept around competence: learning, practicing, contributing, belonging, and seeing that effort can produce improvement. The Almanac should therefore emphasize **earned competence without tying worth to performance**.

## Parent mission
Create a home environment where the child can practice real skills, make meaningful contributions, experience manageable difficulty, receive useful feedback, and remain secure when performance is imperfect.

## Major developmental domains
- Academic: literacy, numeracy, learning strategies, curiosity, school habits
- Character: persistence, honesty, fairness, responsibility, integrity
- Social: friendship, cooperation, conflict, belonging, perspective-taking
- Emotional: emotion identification, coping, frustration tolerance, self-concept
- Physical: movement, coordination, sleep, nutrition, body confidence, safety
- Responsibility: chores, organization, time, possessions, commitments
- Creativity: making, imagining, experimenting, artistic expression
- Practical life: self-care, food, money, household competence, community navigation

## Developmental tasks
1. Build foundational academic competence.
2. Learn that practice can improve performance.
3. Develop persistence without equating persistence with never stopping.
4. Increase responsibility for belongings, routines, and contributions.
5. Learn to participate in groups and negotiate social problems.
6. Develop a more differentiated self-concept: "I am good at some things and still learning others."
7. Internalize increasingly complex rules and principles.
8. Develop strategies for managing frustration, disappointment, and mistakes.
9. Expand independent problem-solving while retaining access to adult support.
10. Develop interests through repeated practice and exploration.

## Important tensions
- Independence vs. scaffolding
- Achievement vs. intrinsic motivation
- Competition vs. cooperation
- Correction vs. confidence
- Responsibility vs. overload
- Rules vs. reasoning
- Persistence vs. knowing when to change strategy
- Peer belonging vs. individuality

## Parent stance
The parent should increasingly move from **doing for** toward **teaching, scaffolding, and stepping back**. The child should experience real responsibility while knowing that help remains available.

## Failure modes to avoid
- Making performance the measure of worth
- Rescuing too quickly
- Praising ability instead of process and strategy
- Turning every interest into an achievement project
- Comparing siblings or peers
- Treating normal developmental variation as deficiency
- Overloading the child with organized activities
- Using shame as a behavior-management tool
- Confusing obedience with maturity

## Healthy trajectory
A healthy trajectory is not "the child becomes excellent at everything." It is increasingly:
- willing to try
- able to practice
- able to ask for help
- able to recover from mistakes
- able to contribute
- able to cooperate
- increasingly responsible
- increasingly aware of strengths and areas for growth

## Warning-sign principle
Warning signs must remain observational and non-diagnostic. The corpus should describe patterns that warrant attention, especially persistent, severe, regressive, or cross-setting difficulties, and route the parent toward appropriate professional or school support rather than labeling the child.

## Corpus implication
Entries should answer one useful question at a time. The same developmental principle may legitimately appear in several categories only when its function changes. For example, persistence can become an `experiencing` observation, a `teach` capability, an `activity`, a `life_skill`, and a `parent_reminder`—but those entries must provide distinct utility rather than paraphrases.
