## Category-specific QA interpretation

### Experiencing
Use this category for developmental realities the parent may observe but misunderstand. Avoid turning milestone observations into prescriptions.

### Needs
Describe conditions that support development: responsive interaction, safe exploration, movement, sleep, routines, and manageable choice. Avoid implying that every child requires the exact same behavior or schedule.

### Teach
Focus on capabilities that can be modeled and practiced: simple communication, safety language, self-care participation, imitation, turn-taking, and repair.

### Encourage
Separate what parents can actively invite from what children are merely expected to achieve. Favor invitations over pressure.

### Avoid
Prioritize safety, respectful discipline, body autonomy, realistic expectations, and prevention of unnecessary power struggles.

### Healthy Development
Use observable examples and normal-variation language. Do not treat a single absent milestone as a diagnosis.

### Warning Signs
Use persistent, marked, regressive, painful, or function-limiting concerns as the trigger for professional discussion. Do not diagnose from an Almanac entry.

### Weekly / Monthly Focus
Convert development into manageable family experiments rather than compliance programs.

### Books / Activities / Conversations
Choose material that produces interaction and exploration. The point is developmental opportunity, not worksheet completion.

### Life Skills
Treat independence as participation first, mastery later. Supervision remains appropriate.

### Opportunities
These are experience ideas, not achievement requirements. Local availability and child temperament vary.

### Parent Reminders
Keep the parent perspective central: connection, pacing, modeling, safety, and tolerance for messy learning.

## Evidence anchors

CDC currently publishes age-specific milestone resources at 15 months, 18 months, and 2 years and defines milestones as things most children (75% or more) can do by a given age. CDC also states that its milestone checklists are not substitutes for standardized developmental screening. The AAP recommends ongoing developmental surveillance and standardized developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months, with autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months.
