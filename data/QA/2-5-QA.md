## Category-specific QA interpretation

### Experiencing
Use this category to explain the developmental season: autonomy, language, pretend play, peer interest, emerging perspective-taking, attention, and the gap between understanding and reliable self-control.

### Needs
Emphasize responsive relationships, predictable routines, play, movement, language-rich interaction, sleep, nutrition, and safe independence without prescribing one universal parenting style.

### Teach
Teach functional skills and concepts through modeling and practice: emotional vocabulary, help-seeking, sequencing, self-care, safety, early literacy, early numeracy, and repair.

### Encourage
Favor curiosity, persistence, imaginative play, contribution, questions, pattern-finding, movement, creativity, and social participation.

### Avoid
Prioritize non-violent discipline, reduced shame, realistic expectations, safe boundaries, body autonomy, and avoiding unnecessary mealtime or behavioral power struggles.

### Healthy Development
Describe observable patterns across domains rather than using milestone lists as pass/fail tests.

### Warning Signs
Use persistent, marked, regressive, painful, or function-limiting concerns as triggers to seek professional guidance. Do not diagnose from the Almanac.

### Weekly / Monthly Focus
Frame these as family themes and repeated practice, not performance plans.

### Books / Activities / Conversations
Use reading, play, and conversation as interactive developmental environments. Favor active participation over passive completion.

### Life Skills
Build competence through contribution and repetition while matching task difficulty to the child's abilities and supervision needs.

### Opportunities
Treat outings, classes, cultural experiences, nature, arts, and movement as optional developmental experiences rather than achievement requirements.

### Parent Reminders
The parent is part of the developmental environment. Pace, modeling, boundaries, repair, and opportunities for safe struggle matter as much as the child's immediate behavior.

## Evidence anchors

CDC currently publishes age-specific milestone resources at 2 years, 30 months, 3 years, 4 years, and 5 years and defines milestones as things most children (75% or more) can do by a given age. CDC also states that its milestone checklists are not substitutes for standardized developmental screening. The AAP recommends ongoing developmental surveillance, standardized developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months, autism-specific screening at 18 and 24 months, and continued developmental surveillance around the 4- or 5-year visit.
